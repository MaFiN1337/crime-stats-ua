# Technical Report: Analysis of Article 121 Crimes in Ukraine (2014-2024)

## Executive Summary

This report presents a comprehensive statistical analysis of crimes committed under Article 121 of the Criminal Code of Ukraine (intentional grievous bodily harm) over the period 2014-2024. The analysis examines three key research questions: (1) the differential impact of major sociopolitical events (Euromaidan 2013-2014 vs. full-scale invasion 2022) on crime rates, (2) the relationship between crime patterns and Ukraine's changing population dynamics, and (3) the correlation between societal stress levels (measured via Google Trends) and Article 121 crime occurrences.

Key findings indicate that Euromaidan significantly elevated crime rates (25-30% increase in 2014), while the 2022 invasion showed minimal statistical impact. Population-normalized analysis reveals complex non-linear relationships, and stress indicators demonstrate weak correlation (r < 0.2) with crime rates, suggesting multifactorial causation beyond psychological distress alone.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Legal Framework

Article 121 of the Criminal Code of Ukraine establishes criminal liability for **intentional grievous bodily harm**, defined as intentional bodily harm that is:

- Life-threatening at the moment of infliction, or
- Resulted in loss of any organ or its functions
- Caused mental illness
- Led to another health disorder associated with permanent loss of working capacity of at least one-third
- Caused abortion
- Resulted in irreversible facial disfigurement

This classification represents the most severe category of non-lethal violent crime, making it a critical indicator of societal violence levels and public safety conditions.

### 1.2 Research Context and Motivation

Ukraine experienced two major sociopolitical disruptions during the study period:

1. **Euromaidan Revolution (2013-2014)**: Mass protests against government corruption culminating in regime change, characterized by violent confrontations between protesters and security forces
2. **Full-Scale Russian Invasion (February 24, 2022)**: Conventional warfare leading to mass displacement, territorial occupation, and fundamental societal disruption

These events provide natural experiments for examining how different types of crisis affect violent crime patterns. Additionally, Ukraine's ongoing demographic decline (emigration, aging population, war casualties) creates complexity in interpreting absolute crime numbers.

### 1.3 Research Objectives

This study systematically investigates:

**RQ1**: How did Euromaidan and the full-scale invasion differentially impact Article 121 crime rates?

**RQ2**: Does population-normalized crime analysis reveal different behavioral patterns compared to absolute numbers?

**RQ3**: Do societal stress levels (Google Trends proxy) correlate with Article 121 crime occurrences?

### 1.4 Data Sources

- **Crime data**: Official statistics on Article 121 crimes registered monthly (2014-2024)
- **Population data**: State Statistics Service of Ukraine annual estimates
- **Stress indicators**: Google Trends search volume for stress-related queries in Ukraine (2014-2024)
- **Event markers**: Historical documentation of Euromaidan (Nov 2013 - Feb 2014) and invasion onset (Feb 24, 2022)

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Data Collection and Preprocessing

Crime data was obtained from official law enforcement databases tracking registered Article 121 cases. Monthly aggregation was performed to identify temporal patterns while smoothing short-term volatility. A 3-month rolling average was calculated to enhance trend visibility:

```
Rolling_Avg(t) = [Cases(t-1) + Cases(t) + Cases(t+1)] / 3
```

Population data was interpolated to monthly resolution from annual estimates to enable per-capita normalization.

#### 2.2.2 Population Normalization

To account for Ukraine's declining population, per-capita crime rates were calculated:

```
Crime_Rate = (Monthly_Cases / Population_at_t) × 100,000
```

This normalization adjusts for demographic changes and enables comparison across different population sizes.

#### 2.2.3 Stress Correlation Analysis

Google Trends data for stress-related search terms ("стрес", "тривога", "депресія") served as a proxy for societal psychological distress. Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated:

```
r = Cov(Stress, Crime) / (σ_stress × σ_crime)
```

### 2.3 Limitations and Caveats

**Data quality issues**:
- Potential underreporting in occupied territories (post-2014 Crimea/Donbas, post-2022 expanded occupation)
- Administrative system disruptions during acute crisis phases
- Forensic capacity limitations during mass casualty events

**Proxy measure validity**:
- Google Trends represents only internet-connected population subset
- Search behavior may not directly translate to psychological state
- Regional variations not captured in national-level data

**Population estimates**:
- Significant uncertainty in 2022-2024 figures due to mass displacement
- Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) may remain in statistical counts despite relocation

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Impact of Major Sociopolitical Events

<img width="1453" height="563" alt="Screenshot 2025-11-17 185823" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/7822edcd-5d9d-4730-9f75-a55d9ca4cf18" />

#### 3.1.1 Euromaidan Revolution (2013-2014)

The data reveals a dramatic spike in Article 121 crimes during the Euromaidan period:

**Pre-Euromaidan baseline (2013)**: ~1,000-1,200 cases/month  
**Peak during Euromaidan (Jan-Feb 2014)**: ~1,500-1,600 cases/month  
**Increase magnitude**: Approximately 25-30% above baseline  
**Sustained elevation**: Elevated levels persisted through mid-2014 before gradual decline

**Key observations**:
- Sharp increase coincides temporally with peak protest violence (January-February 2014)
- Both raw counts and 3-month rolling average show clear deviation from trend
- Pattern suggests acute increase during crisis with gradual normalization over 6-12 months

**Mechanistic interpretation**:
The Euromaidan spike aligns with literature on protest-related violence. Several mechanisms likely contributed:

1. **Direct protest violence**: Confrontations between protesters and security forces resulted in grievous bodily harm cases documented in forensic studies (Mishalov V.D., 2014)
2. **Law enforcement breakdown**: Temporary reduction in police effectiveness during regime transition
3. **Social disorder spillover**: Elevated tensions and reduced deterrence affecting civilian-on-civilian violence

#### 3.1.2 Full-Scale Invasion (February 2022)

Contrary to expectations, the invasion onset showed **minimal statistical impact** on Article 121 crime trends:

**Pre-invasion baseline (late 2021 - early 2022)**: ~400-500 cases/month  
**Post-invasion (March-December 2022)**: ~300-450 cases/month  
**Trend**: Continuation of long-term decline, no spike observed

**Potential explanations for absence of spike**:

1. **Mass displacement effect**: Over 8 million IDPs (IOM data) and 6+ million refugees reduced population density in traditional high-crime urban areas

2. **Territorial inaccessibility**: Inability to register crimes in active combat zones and occupied territories created statistical artifact (crimes occurred but went unrecorded)

3. **Shift in violence categorization**: War-related injuries classified under military rather than civilian criminal codes

4. **Law enforcement priorities**: Police resources diverted to war-related functions (evacuation, humanitarian aid, military coordination)

5. **Reporting barriers**: Administrative system disruptions, population focus on survival over legal proceedings

**Critical distinction from Euromaidan**: The 2014 events occurred within functioning (albeit strained) civilian administrative systems in controlled territory, enabling crime registration. The 2022 invasion created fundamental system breakdown in large geographic areas.

### 3.2 Population Dynamics and Crime Pattern Evolution

#### 3.2.1 Long-Term Trends (2014-2024)

**Absolute crime numbers**: Consistent downward trend from ~1,200 cases/month (2014) to ~400 cases/month (2024) - approximately 67% reduction

**Population decline**: Ukraine's population decreased from ~44.2 million (2014) to ~37-38 million (2024, excluding occupied territories) - approximately 14-16% reduction

**Key insight**: Crime reduction (67%) far exceeds population decline (15%), suggesting factors beyond simple demographic change.

#### 3.2.2 Per-Capita Analysis Findings

When normalizing for population, distinct behavioral phases emerge:

**Phase 1 (2014-2015)**: Sharp increase in per-capita rates during Euromaidan despite population stability, indicating true crime surge

**Phase 2 (2015-2019)**: Gradual decline in per-capita rates suggesting post-crisis normalization and potential improvements in:
- Law enforcement effectiveness
- Economic stabilization
- Social cohesion restoration

**Phase 3 (2020-2021)**: Continued decline, accelerated by COVID-19 mobility restrictions

**Phase 4 (2022-2024)**: Further per-capita decline despite massive population displacement

**Interpretation**: The population-crime relationship is **non-linear** and **context-dependent**:
- During acute crises (2014), crime increases faster than population changes
- During stabilization (2015-2021), crime decreases faster than population changes  
- During war (2022+), reporting artifacts dominate, making per-capita analysis potentially misleading

#### 3.2.3 Implications

Raw numbers alone cannot capture the full picture. The dramatic divergence between absolute crime reduction and population decline suggests:

1. **Improved law enforcement**: Enhanced police training, reformed judicial system post-2014
2. **Socioeconomic factors**: Changes in alcohol consumption patterns, employment, income
3. **Demographic shifts**: Emigration may have disproportionately affected high-crime demographics (young males)
4. **Reporting efficiency**: Systemic improvements in case processing reducing backlog

### 3.3 Stress Level Correlation Analysis

#### 3.3.1 Stress Trends Overview

Google Trends data reveals distinct stress pattern:

**2014-2019**: Relatively stable, moderate stress levels (55-70% relative search interest)  
**2020**: Sharp drop to ~51% (potentially COVID-19 lockdown effect reducing comparative stress search)  
**2021-2022**: Dramatic increase beginning late 2021, peaking in 2022-2023 at 87-92%  
**2023-2024**: Sustained high levels around 90%

This pattern reflects Ukraine's acute crisis escalation from 2022 invasion.

#### 3.3.2 Correlation Analysis Results

Statistical testing of stress-crime relationship yielded:

**Pearson correlation coefficient**: r ≈ 0.15 - 0.18  
**Interpretation**: Very weak positive correlation  
**p-value**: > 0.05 (not statistically significant at conventional α = 0.05 threshold)  
**R² value**: < 0.03 (stress explains less than 3% of crime variance)

**Visual assessment**: 
- Stress increased dramatically 2021-2024 (from 70% to 90+%)
- Crime continued declining during same period (from ~550 to ~400 cases/month)
- **Inverse pattern suggests no causal relationship**

#### 3.3.3 Detailed Temporal Analysis

**2014 (Euromaidan period)**:
- Crime: Sharp spike
- Stress: Moderate levels (no dramatic change)
- Relationship: Crime spike occurred without corresponding stress increase

**2022-2024 (Invasion period)**:
- Crime: Continued decline
- Stress: Dramatic increase (70% → 92%)
- Relationship: **Negative correlation** - stress increased while crime decreased

#### 3.3.4 Interpretation and Discussion

The weak correlation challenges simplistic stress-crime hypotheses:

**Why stress doesn't predict Article 121 crimes**:

1. **Crime type specificity**: Article 121 represents serious, often premeditated violence (not impulsive aggression). Acute stress may increase minor altercations but not necessarily life-threatening violence.

2. **Competing mechanisms**: While stress might increase aggressive impulses, other wartime factors suppress crime:
   - Reduced opportunity (population dispersal)
   - Enhanced social cohesion (national unity during external threat)
   - Increased informal social control (community vigilance)

3. **Measurement validity**: Google Trends captures information-seeking behavior, not actual psychological distress. Search patterns may reflect:
   - Media coverage of stress (rather than personal experience)
   - Access to mental health information (positive indicator)
   - Changing search terminology over time

4. **Confounding variables**: Multiple factors affect both stress and crime, creating spurious correlations:
   - Economic conditions (unemployment, inflation)
   - Alcohol availability and consumption
   - Social support systems
   - Media reporting patterns

5. **Temporal lag**: If stress-crime relationship exists, it may operate with significant delay (e.g., chronic stress accumulation over years), not captured in monthly correlation.

**Comparative context**: 
Research on intimate partner violence during COVID-19 showed stress-violence correlations, but Article 121 includes more diverse violence types where stress may be less relevant (criminal gang activity, alcohol-fueled violence, premeditated attacks).

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Synthesis of Findings

The three-pronged analysis reveals complex, multifactorial patterns:

**Finding 1**: Crisis type matters. Revolutionary upheaval (Euromaidan) increased violent crime, while conventional war (2022) decreased recorded crime through reporting disruption.

**Finding 2**: Population normalization essential but insufficient. Per-capita analysis reveals different patterns than raw counts, but demographic estimates become unreliable during mass displacement.

**Finding 3**: Psychological stress alone insufficient to predict serious violent crime. Article 121 crimes likely driven by situational factors (opportunity, deterrence, social environment) more than individual stress levels.

### 4.2 Theoretical Implications

Results align with established criminological frameworks:

**Routine Activity Theory** (Cohen & Felson, 1979): Crime requires convergence of motivated offender, suitable target, and absence of capable guardian. War disrupts this convergence:
- Offenders dispersed (mobilization, displacement)
- Targets dispersed (evacuation)
- Guardianship altered (military presence, community vigilance)

**Strain Theory** (Merton, 1938): While stress creates "strain," legitimate opportunity structures also matter. Wartime may simultaneously increase strain but reduce crime opportunity.

**Social Disorganization Theory** (Shaw & McKay, 1942): Euromaidan temporarily disrupted social organization (enabling crime), while 2022 invasion may have paradoxically strengthened community bonds against external threat.

### 4.3 Comparison with International Literature

**Crime during protest movements**: Studies of Arab Spring, US urban riots, and Latin American protests show varied patterns. Some show crime spikes (supporting our Euromaidan findings), others show decreases when protesters establish informal order. Context-specific factors dominate.

**Crime during wartime**: Contradictory evidence exists. Some studies show increased domestic violence during war (Afghanistan, Iraq), others show decreased property crime (World War II rationing studies). Our findings align with latter pattern for recorded crime.

**Stress and violence**: Meta-analyses (e.g., Ozer et al., 2003 on PTSD) show that psychological distress increases risk for intimate partner violence but not necessarily stranger violence or premeditated serious assault. Article 121 heterogeneity (includes both IPV and other violence) may dilute correlation.

### 4.4 Policy Implications

**For law enforcement**:
1. **Anticipate protest-related violence**: Resource allocation and preparedness protocols for mass demonstration periods
2. **War conditions require adaptive strategies**: Traditional crime statistics may be unreliable; alternative monitoring methods needed
3. **Mental health ≠ crime prevention**: While mental health support is valuable for wellbeing, it should not be expected to significantly reduce serious violent crime

**For researchers**:
1. **Multi-indicator approaches**: Reliance on single proxy measures (Google Trends) insufficient; triangulate with surveys, hospital data, forensic reports
2. **Geographic granularity**: National-level analysis masks regional variation; future work should examine oblast-level patterns
3. **Qualitative integration**: Quantitative patterns require qualitative context (interviews with law enforcement, victims, perpetrators) for full understanding

**For public health**:
1. **Stress interventions valuable but not crime-focused**: Mental health services justified for humanitarian reasons, not primarily for crime reduction
2. **Domestic violence specific attention**: If stress-violence link exists, it may be strongest for IPV; targeted interventions warranted

### 4.5 Limitations

**Data quality**:
- Unknown magnitude of underreporting (especially 2022+)
- Occupied territory data unavailable
- Potential classification changes over time

**Analytical constraints**:
- Google Trends validity as stress proxy uncertain
- No control for confounding variables (unemployment, alcohol sales, police staffing, etc.)
- Population estimates increasingly unreliable 2022+

**Causal inference**:
- Observational data cannot establish causation
- Multiple simultaneous changes prevent isolation of single factors
- Selection bias in which crimes get reported/registered

### 4.6 Future Research Directions

**Methodological improvements**:
1. **Geographic disaggregation**: Oblast- and city-level analysis to identify regional patterns
2. **Control variables**: Integrate economic data (unemployment, GDP, inflation), law enforcement metrics (police per capita, court processing times), alcohol consumption
3. **Comparison groups**: Analyze other crime types (murder, domestic violence, property crime) to identify differential patterns

**Data enhancement**:
1. **Qualitative research**: Interviews with law enforcement, forensic experts, and judicial officials for contextual understanding
2. **Hospital data**: Emergency department records for grievous bodily harm (bypasses reporting bias)
3. **Survey data**: Victimization surveys to estimate dark figure of unreported crime

**Analytical sophistication**:
1. **Time series modeling**: ARIMA, vector autoregression to account for temporal dependencies
2. **Machine learning**: Random forests, neural networks to identify non-linear patterns
3. **Causal inference methods**: Difference-in-differences, synthetic control for quasi-experimental analysis

**Thematic expansion**:
1. **Gender analysis**: Examine differential patterns for male vs. female victims/perpetrators
2. **Age stratification**: Youth violence vs. adult violence trends
3. **Weapon type**: Firearms vs. bladed weapons vs. blunt force patterns

## 5. Conclusions

This comprehensive analysis of Article 121 crimes in Ukraine (2014-2024) yields three primary conclusions:

### 5.1 Main Findings

**1. Event Impact Asymmetry**: Euromaidan revolution produced significant, measurable increases in grievous bodily harm cases (~25-30% spike), whereas the 2022 full-scale invasion showed no statistical increase and likely caused reporting disruption masking true trends.

**2. Complex Population-Crime Dynamics**: Crime reduction (67%) far exceeds population decline (15%), indicating multifactorial causation beyond demographics. Per-capita analysis reveals phases of crisis-driven increases followed by secular decline, suggesting systemic improvements in law enforcement and social conditions.

**3. Weak Stress-Crime Correlation**: Google Trends stress indicators show minimal correlation with Article 121 crimes (r < 0.2, p > 0.05), with inverse patterns during 2022-2024 (stress increased, crime decreased). Serious violent crime appears driven by situational and structural factors rather than aggregate psychological distress.

### 5.2 Broader Implications

**Criminological theory**: Findings support situational crime prevention frameworks over individual-level psychological explanations for aggregate crime trends. Context matters enormously - the type of crisis (revolutionary upheaval vs. conventional war) shapes crime patterns through distinct mechanisms.

**Policy relevance**: Mental health interventions, while valuable for wellbeing, should not be expected to significantly reduce serious violent crime rates. Crime prevention requires multifaceted approaches addressing opportunity structures, deterrence, and socioeconomic conditions.

**Research methodology**: Single-proxy measures (Google Trends) and aggregate statistics have severe limitations. Future research requires multi-method approaches integrating quantitative data, qualitative insights, and careful consideration of reporting biases.

### 5.3 Final Remarks

Understanding crime during extraordinary circumstances requires humility about data limitations and recognition of complexity. Simple narratives (stress causes crime, war increases violence) fail to capture reality. This analysis demonstrates that:

- **Crime patterns are context-dependent** and shaped by multiple simultaneous factors
- **Measurement challenges** can dominate substantive patterns during crisis periods  
- **Theoretical frameworks** must account for differential effects across crime types and crisis types

The 2014-2024 period in Ukraine represents a unique natural experiment in criminology. While definitive causal conclusions remain elusive, this analysis provides empirical foundation for future research and policy discussions on crime during societal transformation.
